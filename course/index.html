Step 1 of 6

Phishing, smishing & vishing

Three names for one idea: a message pretending to be someone you trust, built to make you act before you think. It arrives by email, by text, or as a very calm voice on the phone.

Key ideas

Phishing — email

A spoofed or lookalike domain, a reason to hurry, and a link to a login page that is not the real one. The page can be a pixel-perfect copy; the address never is.

Smishing — SMS and chat apps

"Your payment failed", "your package is held", "update your bank details" — almost always with a shortened link, from a number you do not know.

Vishing — phone calls

Someone friendly and unhurried claiming to be IT, the bank, or a client, walking you toward reading out a code or approving a prompt on your phone.

What they are actually after

Your password, your two-factor code, a payment sent somewhere new, or a file opened on your machine. Everything else in the message is decoration.

The five signs

Urgency or fear Odd sender address Unexpected link or file Asking for a secret "Skip the usual process"

Bad grammar is no longer a reliable tell — plenty of these are perfectly written. The pressure to act quickly is the signal that survives.

Step 2 of 6

Impersonation & urgent requests

The most expensive attacks never break anything. They ask politely, as someone you already trust, on a day you are already busy.

Key ideas

It starts with research, not hacking

Public profiles say who your client is, who you report to, and who handles payments. That is enough to write a message that sounds exactly right.

The disguise is usually thin

A lookalike domain, a personal address instead of the work one, or "this is my new number, I lost my phone". The name looks familiar; the channel is new.

Authority + urgency + secrecy

Someone important, something that cannot wait, and a reason not to mention it to anyone. That combination is the attack, almost every time.

Watch anything that moves money or data

Gift cards, wire transfers, updated bank details, payroll changes, "send me the customer list". New payment details always get confirmed by voice.

The one rule that covers all of it: verify out-of-band. Confirm the request on a channel the request did not come from, using contact details you already had — never the number or address inside the message.

Step 3 of 6

Passwords, 2FA & account access

Most account takeovers are not clever. They are an old password, leaked from some unrelated website years ago, tried patiently against everything else you own.

Key ideas

Length beats cleverness

Twelve characters minimum, and more is better. Four unrelated words are easier to remember and far harder to crack than one word with symbols swapped in.

Never reuse — not even a variation

Reuse turns one forgotten breach into all of your accounts at once. Adding a number at the end does not count as a different password.

Use a password manager

It is the only realistic way to hold dozens of unique passwords. A notes file, a spreadsheet or a browser full of saved logins with no master password are not the same thing.

Two-factor on everything, email first

Email is the master key — whoever holds it can reset every other account. An app-based code or a security key is stronger than SMS.

Nobody legitimate needs your code

Not IT, not support, not your client, not a colleague. A request for a password or a one-time code is, by itself, proof that something is wrong.

Step 4 of 6

Devices, browsers & downloads

Your work machine is a client's office. Whoever sits at it — or whatever gets installed on it — is inside their business.

Key ideas

Lock the screen, every time

Windows key + L, or Control + Command + Q on a Mac. Also at home, also for a minute, also around people you trust completely. It costs one second.

Updates are the cheapest defence you have

Operating system, browser, and the extensions you forgot you installed. Most successful attacks use a hole that was patched months earlier.

Trusted sites get compromised too

Attackers infect the sites a group of people visit anyway, then wait. A familiar site is not proof of safety — what the site asks you to do is the signal.

Install nothing on a web page's say-so

"Install this plugin to continue", "your video player is out of date", "run this to verify you are human". Legitimate sites never need any of it.

Read the domain, not the page

Look at the words immediately before the first slash. Everything to the left can be faked, and a perfect copy of a login screen takes minutes to build.

Step 5 of 6

Client data & confidentiality

Customer lists, invoices, ID documents, contact details — that information belongs to the client, wherever it happens to be sitting. Including on your desktop.

Key ideas

Know what counts as personal data

Names, emails, phone numbers, addresses, payment details, ID and tax numbers, health information — and less obvious things like IP addresses and device identifiers.

Take the minimum, keep it the minimum time

Data you never collected cannot leak. Neither can the export you deleted when the task was finished. "I might need it later" is how copies multiply.

Approved tools only — AI included

Pasting a customer list, a contract or internal documents into an unapproved chatbot is a disclosure to a third party. Check before you use a new tool, not after.

Nothing through personal accounts

No personal email, no personal cloud drive, no photos of screens on your own phone. Once client data is in your personal space, nobody can protect it or delete it properly.

Need-to-know, with named access

Share with the people the work requires, through their own accounts. Shared logins hide who did what, which is exactly what you need to know when something goes wrong.

Try it

Where does each of these belong?

0 of 4 placed

Four things that turn up in a normal week. Pick the right home for each.

A customer export — 4,000 names and email addresses

You generated it to clean up a mailing list.

It stays inside the client's own tools, where their access controls, logging and backups apply. It should never travel by email attachment or personal drive.

The client's social account login and recovery codes

Handed to you so you can publish on their behalf.

Credentials belong in a password manager — never in a chat thread, a spreadsheet or a notes app. Better still, ask to be added as your own named user instead of sharing one login.

A photo of a credit card a customer sent into the chat

They were trying to be helpful and pay faster.

Card details must never be stored or forwarded. Point the customer to the proper payment link, remove the image, and let the client know it came in that way.

Your own copy of that export, saved on your desktop

You kept it "just in case", so the work goes faster next time.

This is the copy nobody knows about, so nobody protects it. It survives the project, the laptop and the client relationship. Work from the original and delete the local copy.

✓
That is the pattern. Client data lives in client systems, credentials live in a password manager, and anything else is a copy waiting to become a problem. If you are unsure where something belongs, ask before you save it — that question is never unwelcome.

Step 6 of 6

Client accounts & speaking up

Shared accounts are where access quietly accumulates. And a small problem stays small only for as long as it takes someone to mention it.

Key ideas

Your own named access, at the lowest level that works

Ask to be invited as a user on the client's business tools rather than handed a shared password. Take the permissions the work needs, not administrator "just in case".

Review what is connected

Third-party apps, old scheduling tools, forgotten integrations and sessions still open on a device you no longer use. Each one is a door that stays unlocked.

Hand access back when the work ends

When a task, a client or a role changes, make sure your access is removed and anything you held is handed over properly. Access nobody remembers is access nobody watches.

Say it out loud, immediately

A login alert you do not recognise, a link you clicked and regretted, a message you did not send. Tell your Coconut contact right away — before you are sure, not after.

Speed is the whole strategy

An account secured in ten minutes is an incident. The same account left quiet until tomorrow is a breach. Reporting fast is doing the job well, and it is treated that way.
